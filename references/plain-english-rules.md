## Omit surplus words

### Revise bad construction of a sentence

Definitions:

A sentence typically has two kinds of words: working words and glue words. Working words carry the meaning of the sentence. Glue words hold words together to form a sentence, but they do not convey meaning.

Example: in the sentence "The working words carry the meaning of the sentence", working words include "working", "words", "carry", "meaning" and "sentence"; glue words include "the", "the", "of" and "the".

Rules:

Carefully construct the sentence, and try to use as few glue words as possible. When there are too many glue words in a sentence, take it apart and reshape the pieces to fit together tighter.

Examples:

Original: A trial by jury was requested by the defendant. (working words: trial, jury, requested, defendant; glue words: a, by, was, by, the)

Revised: The defendant requested a jury trial. (working words: defendant, requested, jury, trial; glue words: the, a)

Original: The ruling by the trial judge was prejudicial error for the reason that it cut off cross-examination with respect to issues that were vital. (working words: ruling, trial judge, prejudicial error, reason, cut off, cross-examination, issues, vital; glue words: the, by the, was, for the, that, it, with respect to, that were)

Revised: The trial judge's ruling was prejudicial error because it cut off cross-examination on vital issues. (working words: trial judge's ruling, prejudicial error, cut off, cross-examination, vital issues; glue words: the, was, because, it, on)

### Focus on the Actor, the Action and the Object

Identify the actor, the action and the object and state them.

Examples:

Original: It is possible for the court to modify the judgment.

Revised: The court can modify the judgment.
Original: It is obvious that the summons was not properly served.
Revised: Obviously the summons was not properly served.

### Do not use redundant legal phrases

Replace 60% of redundant legal phrases with simple words. Below are examples: alter or change; last will and testament; confessed and acknowledged; made and entered into; convery, transfer and set over; order and direct; for and during the period; peace and quiet; force and effect; rest, residue, and remainder; free and clear; save and except; full and complete; suffer or permit; give, devise and bequeath; true and correct; good and sufficient; undertake and agree

## Use base verbs, not nominalizations

Use base verbs as much as possible, only use their nominalizations where necessary. For example, use "reject" rather than "rejection", use "imply" rather than "implication", etc.

## Prefer the active voice

Using a passive voice often adds more glue words in a sentence than using an active voice. For example, "John kicked the ball" contains four words; "The ball was kicked by John" contains six words.

Also, passive words can create ambiguity. It may hide the actor. For example, "A ball was kicked" does not say anything about "WHO" kicked the ball; "All improvements of the patended invention that are made hereafter shall promptly be disclosed" does not say "WHO" should disclose.

Therefore, use active voice, only use passive voice where necessary or the user uses it to convey a specific meaning or a specific tone.

Examples:

Original: A complaint was filed by the union.

Revised: The union filed a complaint.

Original: Your motion will be denied by the trial judge.

Revised: The trial judge will deny your motion.

## Use short sentences

Long sentences containing more than one piece of information may frustrate readers. Take the below as an example.

"In a trial by jury, the court may, when the convenience of witnesses or the ends of justice would be promoted thereby, on motion of a party, after notice and hearing, make an order, no later than the close of the pretrial conference in cases in which such pretrial conference is to be held, or in other cases, no later than 10 days before the trial date, that the trial of the issue of liability shall precede the trial of any other issue in the case."

In the above paragraph, there are five pieces of information: 1. In a jury case, the liability issue may be tried before any other issue; 2. the judge may order the liability issue to be tried first if that will serve the convenience of witnesses or the ends of justice; 3. the judge may make the order on a party's motion, after notice and hearing; 4. in a case with a pretrial conference, the judge may make the order no later than the end of the conference; 5. in a case with no pretiral conference, the judge may make the order no later than ten days before the trial date. However, readers have to climb through a thicket of subsidiary idears to arrive the major idea of the passage.

Below is to revise it: In a jury case, the court may order the liability issue to be tried before any other issue. The court may make such an order if doing so serves the convenience of witnesses or the ends of justice. The court may make the order on a party's motion, after notice and hearing. In a case with a pretrial conference, the court may make the order no later than the end of the conference. In a case with no pretrial conference, the court may make the order no later than ten days before the trial date.

Why the revised version is better? It is shorter. Each sentence contains only one main thought, and the thoughs follow in logical sequence.

Keep most sentences short, containing one main idea. Arrange the sentences in a logical sequence.

## Arrange words with care

### Avoid wide gaps between the subject, the verb and the object

Make sure that most sentences follow the normal English word order: first the subject, next the verb, and then the object. Try to put them close together, narrow the gap between the subject, the verb and the object and near the front of the sentence. Below is a revised example:

Original: A claim, which in the case of negligent misconduct shall not exceed $500, and in the case of intentional misconduct shall not exceed $1,000, may be filed with the Office of the Administrator by any injured party.

Revised: Any injured party may file a claim with the Office of the Administrator. Aclaim must not exceed $500 for negligenct misconduct, or $1,000 for intentional misconduct.

### Put conditions and exceptions where they are clear and easy to read

Usually the end of the sentence is the best place for a condition or exeception that is longer than the main clause, and the begining of the sentence is the best place for a condition or exception that is short or there is a need to avoid leading the reader astray. Place conditions and exceptions at the most appropriate place to improve readability and reduce ambiguity. Below are good examples:

Example 1: A lawyer may disclose a client's confidential information if disclosure is necessary to prevent, mitigate, or rectify substantial injury to the financial interests or property of another that is reasonably certain to result or has resulted from the client's commission of a crime or fraud in furtherance of which the client has used the lawyer's services.

Example 2: Except for U.S. citizens, all persons passing this point must have in their possession a valid passport, a baggage clearance certificate, and a yellow entry card.

### When necessary, make a list

If you have to present a cluster of conditions, exceptions or other closely related idears, use a list.

The items in the list must be parallel in substance. For example, bread, eggs and Czar Nicholas II cannot be put into one list.

The items in the list must be parallel in grammar. For example, jurisdiction, venue and preparing charts for Dr. Sullivan's testimony cannot be put into one list.

Use or (if the list is disjunctive) or and (if the list is conjunctive) before the last item in the list.

Put the list at the end of the passage, not at the beginning and not in the middle.

### Put modifying words close to what they modify

Put modifying words as close as you can to the words you want them to modify.

When there is a squinting modifier - one that sits mid-sentence and can be read to modify either what preceds it or what follows it (A trustee who steals dividends often cannot be punished), determine which part it modifies, then choose a word that does not squint, or rearrange the sentence to avoid ambiguity (Usually, a trustee who steals dividends cannot be punished / A trustee who often steals dividends cannot be punished).

Put "only" immediately before the word you want it to modify. If it creates ambiguity in that position, try to isolate it at the beginning or ending of the sentence.

### Avoid nested modifiers

Keep the main clause together. Move nested modifiers into separate sentences, preserving their meaning and scope.

Before: The defendant, who was driving a truck carrying old furniture, some of which was not securely tied down, stopped without warning.

After: The defendant stopped without warning. He was driving a truck carrying old furniture. Some of the furniture was not securely tied down.

### Clarify the reach of modifiers

Make clear which words each modifier applies to. Reorder words, repeat modifiers, or use a list to express the intended scope.

Before: Endangered frogs and salamanders.

After (both endangered): Endangered frogs and endangered salamanders.

After (only frogs endangered): Salamanders and endangered frogs.

## Choose words with care

### Use concret words

Use concrete words to name people, things, actions, and effects. Preserve general terms where their breadth is necessary and intentional.

Before: In our present circumstances, the budgetary aspect is a factor which must be taken into consideration to a greater degree.

After: Now we must think more about money.

### Use familiar words

Choose words familiar to your readers. Prefer simple words when equally precise; use technical or unfamiliar terms when they best fit the audience and meaning.

Before → After: termination → end; expedite → hurry; elucidate → explain; utilize → use.

### In rule drafting, prefer the singular number and the present tense

When drafting rules, prefer the singular and the present tense. Use the plural for groups and other tenses where needed to express timing or the sequence of events.

Before → After: “Persons must not discharge firearms inside city limits.” → “A person must not discharge a firearm inside city limits.”

Before → After: “If contrary information has become available, a supplemental response will be required.” → “If contrary information becomes available, a supplemental response is required.”

## Avoid language quirks

### Avoid elegant variation

Use the same term consistently for the same meaning, repeating it where helpful. Use distinct terms for distinct meanings, especially when they appear close together.

Before: The majority opinion gives no consideration to appellant’s argument that no consideration was given for the promise.

After: The majority opinion ignores appellant’s argument that no consideration was given for the promise.

### Avoid noun chains

Keep only essential words in noun chains. Add prepositions or verbs to make the relationships between nouns clear.

Before: draft laboratory animal rights protection regulations

After: draft regulations to protect the rights of laboratory animals

### Avoid multiple negatives

Express requirements positively. Convert paired negatives into affirmative wording and list multiple conditions separately, preserving their logic and exceptions.

Before: It shall be unlawful to fail to stop at a red light.

After: You must stop at a red light.

### Avoid cosmic detachment

Make people and their actions explicit: identify who does what to whom. Address readers as “you” where appropriate to show how the text applies to them.

Guiding question from the book: “Who is doing what to whom?”

### Use strong nouns and verbs

Where commentary or persuasion is appropriate, use strong, precise nouns and verbs. Choose words whose strength matches the intended meaning and let them carry the emphasis.

Before: The witness intentionally testified untruthfully about the cargo.

After: The witness lied about the cargo.

## Punctuate carefully

### Commas

**Join independent clauses**

Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction (*and, but, or, for, nor, yet, so*) joining two complete sentences. Alternatively, join them with a semicolon.

**Mark introductory elements**

Place a comma after an introductory phrase or clause. Omit it after a very short phrase or before inverted word order; retain it after an introductory dependent clause.

**Set off nonrestrictive elements**

Enclose supplementary information in commas. Keep information essential to identifying the subject or defining the statement’s scope directly connected to the sentence.

**Set off parenthetical elements**

Place commas on both sides of an inserted aside, including an in-text legal citation.

**Separate items in a series**

Separate three or more items with commas, including a comma before the final conjunction. Use semicolons for complex items or items containing commas.

**Separate coordinate adjectives**

Separate adjectives with commas when changing their order or inserting *and* preserves the meaning. Keep cumulative modifiers and the final adjective–noun connection together.

**Examples:** “A dark, cold night”; “A bright red tie”; “A strong constitutional argument.”

**Set off transitions and interruptions**

Use commas around transitional expressions and direct forms of address. When a transition connects two independent clauses, place a semicolon before it and a comma after it.

**Punctuate dates, titles, place names, and short quotations**

In month–day–year dates, set off the year with commas; keep day–month–year dates internally unpunctuated. Set off titles following names and enclosing place names with commas. Introduce a short quotation with a comma; integrate quotations serving as part of the sentence directly.

**Examples:** “Jane Sherwood, M.D., testified for the defense.” / “The witness said, ‘The red car was speeding.’” / “The statute banned smoking ‘in any public building.’”

### Simicolons

**Join closely related independent clauses**

Use a semicolon to join two closely related clauses that can each stand as a complete sentence, without a coordinating conjunction.

**Join independent clauses with a transition**

When a transitional expression such as *therefore, however,* or *for example* connects two independent clauses, place a semicolon before it and a comma after it.

**Separate items in a complex series**

Use semicolons between items when a series is complex or its items contain commas, including before the final conjunction.

### Colons

**Introduce a series**

Place a colon after a complete independent clause to introduce a list. Keep verbs and prepositions directly connected to their objects.

**Introduce a summary, elaboration, or illustration**

Use a colon to introduce material that summarizes, explains, or illustrates the preceding statement. It can also join two independent clauses when the first introduces the second or they express cause and effect.

**Introduce a long quotation**

Use a colon to introduce a quotation longer than one sentence. Ordinarily, use a comma to introduce a short quotation.

### Dashes

**Signal an abrupt break**

Use dashes to emphasize an interruption or abrupt change in thought. Use paired dashes to clearly mark a substantial insertion that must remain beside what it modifies. Choose commas for neutral asides and parentheses for subordinate information.

### Parentheses

**Set off subordinate explanations**

Use parentheses for asides or explanations that deserve less emphasis than the main statement. Punctuate the enclosed material as needed.

**Clarify meaning and scope**

Use parentheses sparingly to clarify what modifies what or to add a brief definition, qualification, or exception.

**Label items in a series**

Use numbers or letters in parentheses—such as (1), (2) or (a), (b)—to mark the divisions in a complex series.

**Introduce shorthand expressions**

Place an abbreviation or shortened term in parentheses after its first full mention, then use it consistently.

### Apostrophes

**Form possessives**

Add *’s* to singular nouns, including those ending in an *s* sound; use an apostrophe alone for a triple *s* sound or classical and biblical names ending in *s*. For plurals, use an apostrophe alone after an *s* sound and *’s* otherwise.

For joint ownership, mark only the last owner; for separate ownership, mark each owner. Attach the possessive to the last element of a compound expression. Write possessive pronouns (*its, whose, theirs*) without apostrophes, and use an *of* phrase when clearer.

**Form plurals of special terms**

Under the book’s convention, use *’s* for plurals of abbreviations, numbers, letters, symbols, and words discussed as words. Form plural years with *s* alone.

**Examples:** “three 6’s”; “mostly B’s”; “the late 1800s.”

**Mark omitted letters**

Use apostrophes for omitted letters in contractions and abbreviations, such as *can’t, it’s,* and *Nat’l*. Use full forms in formal legal documents; use contractions when an informal tone suits the audience and purpose.

### Hyphens

**Check compound terms**

Follow an up-to-date dictionary for whether a compound is written as separate words, a hyphenated term, or one word.

**Hyphenate compound modifiers consistently**

Generally, hyphenate words acting as one modifier before a noun; use open forms after it. Keep phrases beginning with an *-ly* adverb and foreign phrases unhyphenated.

Usually hyphenate *ex-, self-, quasi-,* and *all-*. Join other prefixes directly unless the next element is capitalized or a hyphen clarifies meaning. When compounds share an element, you may state it once: “Long- and short-term budget reductions.”

**Hyphenate compound numbers and fractions**

Hyphenate spelled-out numbers from twenty-one through ninety-nine, including within larger numbers. Hyphenate all elements of a spelled-out fraction: “A one-twenty-sixth share.”

**Divide words at line endings**

When necessary, divide words between syllables with a hyphen, checking dictionary divisions and automatic hyphenation. Keep words intact across page breaks and single-letter syllables attached. Prefer a ragged right margin when practical to reduce word breaks and uneven spacing.

### Periods, question marks, and exclamation points

**End statements and commands with periods**

Use a period to end a declarative sentence, command, or indirect quotation.

**Follow standard abbreviation punctuation**

Follow a dictionary or the applicable citation guide for periods in abbreviations. Use unfamiliar abbreviations only when readers will understand them. Let an abbreviation’s final period also serve as the sentence’s final period.

**End direct questions with question marks**

Use a question mark after a direct question. Use a period after an indirect question or a courteous request phrased as a question.

**Use exclamation points sparingly**

Reserve exclamation points for occasional expressions of surprise or strong emotion in informal writing. Prefer periods in formal legal writing.

### Quotations

**Format direct quotations**

Enclose quotations under fifty words in double quotation marks, alternating double and single marks for nested quotations. Format quotations of fifty words or more as blocks indented on both sides, preserving internal quotation marks. Place commas and periods inside quotation marks; place other punctuation according to whether it belongs to the quotation.

**Mark changes and omissions**

Enclose additions and alterations, including capitalization changes, in square brackets. Mark omissions with spaced ellipsis points (*. . .*). Begin quotations directly, and quote standalone phrases or clauses without ellipses.
